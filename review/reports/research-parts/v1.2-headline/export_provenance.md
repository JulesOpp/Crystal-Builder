## Q1. Can usage be tracked without any watermark (CITATION.cff, DOIs, software-mention mining, in-app "how to cite")? How well does that work for small tools, and what do peer programs do?

### Takeaway
Citation and mention mining are the accepted channel, but they are noisy and biased towards tools that ask for a formal citation of a single canonical reference. Automated mention datasets misclassify a large share of mentions, and only a minority of software use is formally cited. For a small new tool, the biggest lever is to make citing easy and expected: one paper or DOI, a `CITATION.cff`, and an in-app "Cite Crystal Builder" prompt. That is what VESTA and OVITO do, and both are cited at very large scale.

### Cited Findings
- **The CZI Software Mentions dataset** (Istrate, Li, Taraborelli, Torkar, Veytsman, Williams; arXiv 2209.00693, September 2022) holds about 67 million software mentions. They were drawn from about 3.8 M PubMed Central open-access papers and about 16 M publisher-provided papers, and disambiguated into 97,600 software entities from 1.12 M unique mention strings. It is CC0 on Dryad. It covers the **biomedical** literature, so crystallography and materials papers are likely under-represented — [arXiv 2209.00693](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.00693); [CZI announcement](https://medium.com/czi-technology/new-data-reveals-the-hidden-impact-of-open-source-in-science-11cc4a16fea2) (search summary)
- **Mention datasets are unreliable for tracking a specific tool.** Druskat, Chue Hong, Buzzard, Konovalov and Kornek (arXiv 2402.14602, 22 Feb 2024) found that "54% and 23% of mentions respectively are not to software" in the two datasets they evaluated. One dataset has no software links at all. Links exist for only 20.5% of mentions in their sample, and 65.4% of automatically retrieved links point to the wrong software. Their conclusion: "Software should not be mentioned, it should be cited following the software citation principles." — [arXiv 2402.14602](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.14602) (fetched)
- **Informal mentions dominate.** Howison & Bullard (JASIST 67(9):2137–2155, 2016) sampled 90 biology papers. 59 of the 90 (66%) mentioned software, in seven distinct forms: names in the text, URLs in footnotes, various reference-list forms and so on. A related Howison et al. (2015) informant estimated that "less than 10%" of software use results in a citation; that is an anecdote, not a measurement — [EconPapers record](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:jinfst:v:67:y:2016:i:9:p:2137-2155); [arXiv 1706.06527 follow-up](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1706.06527.pdf) (search summary)
- **Mention data are used for the tool-landscape level, not the long tail.** "The software space of science" (Wu & Murray, arXiv 2604.23820, 26 Apr 2026) maps only **520** software tools across 1.3 M publications (2004–2021) into 8 communities. Mining at scale settles on the commonly used tools — [arXiv 2604.23820](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.23820) (fetched)
- **The FORCE11 Software Citation Principles** (Smith, Katz, Niemeyer, PeerJ Comput. Sci. 2:e86, 2016) are the normative basis for asking users to cite software directly — [PeerJ CS 86 proof](https://files.gitter.im/force11/force11-scwg/VpV6/peerj-cs-86-proof.pdf)
- **GitHub reads a `CITATION.cff` in the repository root** and shows a "Cite this repository" button with APA and BibTeX. Zenodo's GitHub integration can mint a DOI per release — [GitHub docs: About CITATION files](https://docs.github.com/en/repositories/managing-your-repositorys-settings-and-features/customizing-your-repository/about-citation-files) (not re-fetched)

**What peer programs do:**
- **VESTA:** the licence says "Drawings produced by VESTA may be used in any publications provided that its use is explicitly acknowledged". The canonical reference is Momma & Izumi, J. Appl. Cryst. 44, 1272–1276 (2011), doi:10.1107/S0021889811038970. This is an **overt, licence-level acknowledgement requirement on figures**, with no embedded mark — [VESTA licence (Gentoo mirror)](https://data.gpo.zugaina.org/gentoo-zh/licenses/VESTA); [Mindat reference](https://www.mindat.org/reference.php?id=4671721) (search summary; licence text is from a third-party mirror)
- **OVITO:** asks users to cite its paper when publishing results, gives a citation block, and markets being "Cited in more than 20,000 research publications, with 10+ new papers every day" — [ovito.org](https://ovito.org/); [OVITO 3.0 intro](https://ovito.org/docs/3.0.0/introduction.php) (search summary)
- **Avogadro, Mercury, CrystalMaker, ASE, pymatgen:** the search did not return their citation or tracking terms (see Gaps).

### Inferences
- Crystal Builder lacks the single most important ingredient VESTA and OVITO have: **one citable reference** (a JOSS or J. Appl. Cryst. paper, or at least a Zenodo concept DOI). Without it, even honest users can only "mention" it, and mentions are what the mining literature shows to be unreliable.
- For a small, new tool, Google Scholar full-text search for the exact string "Crystal Builder" plus the citation count of one canonical DOI will likely find more real usage than any watermark. "Crystal Builder" is also a fairly generic phrase, which argues for a distinctive citation key and a DOI.
- An in-app "Cite" item plus a one-line reminder on export (as VESTA's licence implies, but shown in the UI) is the accepted, low-risk version of what the developer wants.

### Gaps
- Exact citation and usage-tracking terms for Avogadro, Mercury (CCDC), CrystalMaker, ASE and pymatgen were not retrieved in this session.
- I found no study measuring recall of Google Scholar, OpenAlex or Semantic Scholar mention detection specifically for small or new tools.
- GitHub's "Used by" (dependency graph) only counts repositories that declare a dependency. It does not count desktop-app users who publish papers. I did not find a source quantifying this.
- I found no data on whether in-app "please cite" prompts measurably raise citation rates.

## Q2. Structure files: is CIF `_audit_creation_method` / `_computing_*` an accepted provenance channel? How often do deposited CIFs keep it, and has anyone counted program names in deposited CIFs?

### Takeaway
Yes: naming the generating program in a CIF is a long-standing, overt, accepted provenance channel in crystallography. IUCr journals actively curate the `_computing_*` software references. Crystal Builder already writes `_audit_creation_method` and a `# Generated by` comment. I found **no published count** of program names across COD, CSD or journal-SI CIFs, but COD is fully downloadable, so the count can be done directly.

### Cited Findings
- **Crystal Builder already writes these items.** `xtal/io/cif_writer.py` writes `# Generated by …`, `_audit_creation_method` and `_audit_creation_date` (local check of `xtal/io/cif_writer.py`, lines 126–132).
- **`_audit_creation_method`** is a CIF core-dictionary item that records how the CIF was created, for example by naming the program — [IUCr CIF core dictionary](https://www.iucr.org/__data/iucr/cifdic_html/1/cif_core.dic/Iaudit_creation_method.html) (not re-fetched)
- **IUCr journals curate the `_computing_*` software references.** Acta Cryst. C editorial material notes that co-editors "often correct references to software used for diffractometer control, data processing, structure solution and refinement". Standard references exist for common programs and are built into publCIF, and authors must run checkCIF before submission — [Acta Cryst C 2011 editorial (me0438)](https://journals.iucr.org/c/issues/2011/01/00/me0438/); [Acta Cryst C 2010 (me0396)](https://journals.iucr.org/c/issues/2010/01/00/me0396/index.html) (search summary)
- **COD scale and access.** COD has more than 450,000 entries (one source gives >367,000 at time of writing), harvests CIFs from journal websites with crawlers, and was allowed by the IUCr in 2007 to download IUCr-published CIFs freely. Bulk data are available read-only over Subversion and rsync — [COD history paper](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/335604699_Crystallography_Open_Database_History_Development_and_Perspectives); [NAR 2012 COD paper](https://academic.oup.com/nar/article/40/D1/D420/2903497) (search summary)
- **The CSD has been surveyed for quality metrics from CIFs**, showing that deposited CIF metadata is machine-mined at scale. This survey is not about program names — [PMC12573924](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12573924/) (search summary)
- CrystalMaker 11.7.2 (8 Sep 2026) added "enhanced CIF export with creation date, notes, formula and density fields". This shows a commercial peer also strengthening overt CIF provenance — [CrystalMaker release notes](https://crystalmaker.com/crystalmaker/release-notes/mac/11/index.html) (via the sibling notes file `peer_software.md`)

### Inferences
- `_audit_creation_method` will usually **survive** wherever a CIF is deposited unchanged: COD, journal SI, and the CSD deposition copy. It is **overwritten or lost** when the structure is re-processed. Examples are re-refinement in SHELXL or Olex2, which write their own audit and computing blocks, conversion through ASE or pymatgen (which write minimal CIFs), and editing in Mercury or VESTA. So it detects "Crystal Builder made this file" well, and "Crystal Builder was used somewhere upstream" poorly.
- A model or hypothetical structure built in Crystal Builder and deposited as SI is the most likely case where the mark survives. Experimental structures are refined elsewhere, so the mark would correctly be replaced.
- **To quantify retention:** rsync the COD and `grep -l "_audit_creation_method"`, then tally values and `_computing_structure_refinement` values. This is a few hours' work. It would give the base rate of retained creator strings (e.g. SHELXL, Olex2, VESTA, Materials Studio) in deposited CIFs and could be run periodically for "Crystal Builder".
- For XYZ, POSCAR and ORCA inputs, the only overt channel is a comment or title line. Examples are the XYZ second line, the POSCAR first (comment) line and an ORCA `#` comment. These are routinely overwritten by the next program, and journals rarely publish them verbatim.

### Gaps
- **I found no published study counting program names in deposited CIFs** (e.g. the SHELXL share of `_computing_structure_refinement`). The often-cited dominance of SHELX is inferred from Sheldrick's "A short history of SHELX" (Acta Cryst. A64, 112–122, 2008) being among the most-cited papers ever, but I did not fetch a source for that in this session.
- The current IUCr Notes for Authors wording on `_computing_*` was not retrieved; the sources above date from 2010–2014.
- I did not determine whether CCDC's deposition or curation keeps `_audit_creation_method` verbatim in distributed CSD entries.

## Q3. Images: does metadata (PNG tEXt/iTXt, XMP, C2PA) or invisible watermarking survive journal production, and can published PDFs be scanned for it?

### Takeaway
File metadata is overt-in-principle, standard and harmless, but it is fragile. It usually does not survive pasting into a multi-panel figure, PowerPoint or Illustrator, re-export, or publisher re-encoding, and social platforms strip it, C2PA manifests included. Robust invisible watermarks are a research area built for AI-image provenance. Benchmarks show that even the strongest schemes break under regeneration and editing attacks. None is designed for tiny, compositied, vector or rasterised-to-PDF scientific figure panels, and scanning the published literature would mean extracting every image from every PDF. In practice, an invisible watermark in Crystal Builder figures would be expensive, low-recall and high-risk.

### Cited Findings
- **The PNG spec** defines tEXt, zTXt and iTXt chunks with predefined keywords including "Software", "Source" and "Comment". **Matplotlib** writes `Software: Matplotlib version…, https://matplotlib.org/` into PNG metadata by default, and a Creator entry into SVG and PDF. This is a widely accepted precedent of **default, overt** creator metadata in scientific figures — [W3C PNG spec](https://www.w3.org/TR/png-3/); [matplotlib savefig docs](https://matplotlib.org/stable/api/_as_gen/matplotlib.pyplot.savefig.html) (not re-fetched)
- **Metadata stripping on platforms is the norm.** Commentators report that social platforms strip EXIF, XMP, IPTC and C2PA manifests on upload, and that WhatsApp, iMessage and Facebook re-encode images. A Washington Post test found "No platform that The Post tested kept the Content Credentials data". C2PA's answer is the **soft binding**: a watermark or fingerprint that points to a manifest stored in a registry. Adobe's open-source TrustMark was designed for this — [softwareseni: C2PA limits](https://www.softwareseni.com/how-c2pa-content-credentials-work-and-what-their-limits-are/); [softwareseni: durable credentials](https://softwareseni.com/durable-content-credentials-how-provenance-survives-metadata-stripping) (search summary; mostly vendor blogs, specifics unverified)
- **PDF pipelines handle metadata inconsistently.** Ghostscript pdfwrite "doesn't properly replicate all of the input XMP metadata", while PDF/A output must keep its own XMP. Metadata on *embedded images* depends on the pipeline. These are forum and issue sources, not publisher documentation — [OCRmyPDF issue 327](https://github.com/ocrmypdf/OCRmyPDF/issues/327); [Ghostscript.NET issue 117](https://github.com/artifexsoftware/ghostscript.net/issues/117) (search summary)
- **WAVES** (An et al., ICML 2024, arXiv 2401.08573) benchmarks image watermarks against "traditional image distortions to advanced, novel variations of diffusive, and adversarial attacks" and "reveals previously undetected vulnerabilities of several modern watermarking algorithms". The schemes it tests are StegaStamp, Stable Signature and Tree-Ring, with detection reported as TPR at 0.1% FPR, across 26+ attack scenarios — [arXiv 2401.08573](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.08573) (fetched); [WAVES GitHub](https://github.com/umd-huang-lab/waves) (search summary)
- **W-Bench** (arXiv 2410.18775) evaluates watermarks against image *editing*. It finds that StegaStamp and MBRS keep the watermark in some cases "at the expense of image quality" — [arXiv 2410.18775](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.18775v2) (search summary)
- A 2025 ensemble attack network improved attack success against StegaStamp by 18.7% under WAVES's regeneration attack, showing that robustness keeps eroding — [arXiv 2509.03006](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.03006v1) (search summary)
- A 2025 survey covers secure and robust watermarking for AI-generated images. These schemes, such as SynthID and Stable Signature, target **generated photo-like images at full resolution**, not line art or rendered ball-and-stick panels — [arXiv 2510.02384](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.02384) (search summary)

### Inferences
- **Survival through a real publication pipeline**, judged from how the formats work rather than from measured studies:
  - **PNG tEXt and SVG `<metadata>`:** lost as soon as a user composites panels in Illustrator, Inkscape, PowerPoint or Affinity and exports a new file, or when the publisher converts to TIFF, EPS or PDF. They may survive when the raw PNG goes into SI or a data repository.
  - **XMP in PDF:** typically replaced by the publisher's own.
  - **C2PA hard-bound manifests:** stripped by almost any re-encode.
- **Invisible pixel watermarks on Crystal Builder renders face extra hostile conditions.** Panels are small and often downscaled to a few hundred pixels per panel in the PDF. Large flat or white backgrounds give little texture to hide a signal. Labels and arrows get composited on top. SVG is vector and would not carry a pixel watermark at all. Journal PDFs use JPEG or JPEG2000 re-encoding. Expected recall is low, and false positives at literature scale are a real concern.
- **Detecting at scale** would mean harvesting PDFs (licensing and robots constraints apply), extracting embedded images (pdfimages), cropping candidate panels and running a decoder. That is a research project in its own right, with poor yield, compared with a Google Scholar alert for "Crystal Builder".
- **A recognisable visual style** (default palette, lighting, atom shading, an optional small overt logo or caption stamp the user can switch off) is the only image "mark" that reliably survives compositing and print, because it is visible. Crystallographers do recognise VESTA's default look in figures; that is an observation, not a sourced finding. It is overt by nature and raises no integrity issue, but it does not prove provenance.

### Gaps
- I found no study measuring whether PNG, XMP or C2PA metadata in author-supplied figures survives into the published PDF or HTML at specific publishers (Elsevier, ACS, RSC, Springer Nature, IUCr).
- I found no robustness study of invisible watermarks on scientific line art or rendered molecular graphics specifically.
- SynthID and Watermark Anything (Meta) robustness figures were not retrieved in this session.

## Q4. Data files (.xy/CSV PXRD patterns): comment headers versus hidden numerical fingerprints — feasibility, robustness and scientific integrity

### Takeaway
A `# Generated by Crystal Builder x.y (doi…)` comment header is standard, overt and harmless, but it is lost on any re-plot or copy-paste into Origin or Excel. A hidden numerical fingerprint, such as deliberately set low-order digits, changes data values. Even if the change is below instrument precision, it collides with the regulatory definition of falsification ("changing or omitting data"), and it would be indefensible if discovered. It is also not robust: rounding, interpolation, re-binning or normalising destroys it.

### Cited Findings
- **Falsification is defined in regulation.** Under US PHS regulations (42 CFR 93.103(b)), falsification is "manipulating research materials, equipment, or processes, or changing or omitting data or results such that the research is not accurately represented in the research record". A misconduct finding also requires a significant departure from accepted practice, committed intentionally, knowingly or recklessly. HHS revised Part 93 in a final rule of 17 Sep 2024; one secondary source gives an applicable date of 1 Jan 2026, which I did not verify — [42 CFR 93.103 (GPO)](https://www.gpo.gov/fdsys/pkg/CFR-2020-title42-vol1/pdf/CFR-2020-title42-vol1-sec93-103.pdf); [NIH Grants Policy 4.1.27](https://grants.nih.gov/grants/policy/nihgps/HTML5/section_4/4.1.27_research_misconduct.htm) (search summary)
- **Image policy shows how journals treat undisclosed alteration.** Nature Portfolio requires minimally processed images, screens submissions with software, spot-checks randomly selected papers, and asks that unavoidable modifications be described in the legend. The well-known JCB rule is that no specific feature "may be enhanced, obscured, moved, removed or introduced" — [Nature image integrity policy](https://www.nature.com/authors/policies/image.html); [NTU summary quoting JCB](https://ntu.edu.sg/research/resources/image-manipulation) (search summary)

### Inferences
- The scientist would be the one who unknowingly publishes slightly altered data. The developer would then be responsible for introducing changes the user did not make or disclose. Even a calculated (simulated) PXRD pattern is part of the research record once published. Calling it "imperceptible" does not help: integrity rules are about undisclosed alteration, not about magnitude. This option should be ruled out.
- A dither-like fingerprint in *simulated* data is less harmful scientifically than one in measured data, but the same objection applies. It is also trivially destroyed by `np.round(y, 2)` or by re-gridding.
- **Overt alternatives** that do survive and are accepted:
  - a comment header (and the same in the CSV header row);
  - a sidecar JSON or RO-Crate `ro-crate-metadata.json` describing the generating software and version, following W3C PROV `wasGeneratedBy` (W3C PROV and RO-Crate are standard FAIR provenance vocabularies; not re-fetched this session: [W3C PROV-O](https://www.w3.org/TR/prov-o/), [RO-Crate](https://www.researchobject.org/ro-crate/));
  - the existing CIF audit items.

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve RSC, ACS or IUCr image-integrity policy text, or any journal statement specifically on watermarks embedded by software in figures.
- I found no precedent of a scientific program embedding numerical fingerprints in output data, nor of the reaction if one were discovered.

## Q5. Norms, ethics and law on hidden marks and tracking: precedents of controversy or acceptance; licence (MIT) compatibility; GDPR

### Takeaway
The precedents split cleanly. **Overt, disclosed, user-controllable provenance is accepted** (CIF audit fields, Matplotlib's PNG "Software" key, licence-level "acknowledge use" terms such as VESTA's, and Homebrew's documented opt-out analytics). **Hidden identifiers discovered after the fact cause lasting reputational damage**: Microsoft's Word GUID, which helped trace the Melissa author; printer tracking dots, which EFF exposed and which became a privacy cause célèbre; and Audacity's 2021 telemetry and privacy-policy backlash, which led to forks. The MIT licence does not forbid a mark, but anyone can see and remove it. In open source a covert mark is essentially guaranteed to be discovered. If a mark includes any per-user or per-install identifier, EU law (GDPR and ePrivacy) is engaged.

### Cited Findings
**Precedents of hidden identifiers**
- **Microsoft Word GUID / Melissa (1999):** the GUID, "a controversial Microsoft document serial number", was used by Richard Smith of Phar Lap Software to trace the Melissa virus towards its author. Reportedly it embedded the creating PC's network-card MAC address. David L. Smith was arrested on 1 April 1999 — [Network World](https://www.networkworld.com/article/748462/security-melissa-virus-turning-15-age-of-the-stripper-still-unknown.html); [Wikipedia: Melissa](https://Www.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melissa_worm) (search summary)
- **Printer tracking dots:** colour laser printers print near-invisible yellow dots encoding serial number and print date and time. EFF warned about them in 2005 and published a decoding, and said all recent commercial colour laser printers likely print some forensic code. In 2017 the dots were visible on the leaked NSA document linked to Reality Winner, though the arrest affidavit did not cite them — [EFF Deeplinks 2017](https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2017/06/printer-tracking-dots-back-news); [Defense One](https://defenseone.com/technology/2017/06/mysterious-printer-code-may-have-led-fbi-alleged-nsa-leaker/138469) (search summary)
- **Audacity (2021):** after Muse Group acquired the project in May 2021, a telemetry proposal was withdrawn after user pushback. A July 2021 privacy policy got Audacity labelled "possible spyware" and was then revised with an apology. Forks followed, including Tenacity — [TechRadar](https://www.techradar.com/news/audacity-reverses-opt-in-telemetry-plans-following-user-revolt); [BetaNews](https://betanews.com/2021/07/05/audacity-possible-spyware); [Hackaday](https://hackaday.com/2021/07/23/new-privacy-policy-gets-audacity-back-on-track); [It's FOSS (fork)](https://itsfoss.com/audacity-fiasco-fork/) (search summary)

**Precedents of accepted, disclosed tracking**
- **Homebrew:** analytics went opt-in for about a month of testing, then switched to opt-out after mailing-list and Twitter notice, which drew criticism that fresh installs were not informed. They are now documented, don't store IP addresses, are kept 365 days in InfluxDB, and can be switched off with `HOMEBREW_NO_ANALYTICS=1` or `brew analytics off`. Earlier Google Analytics data was destroyed — [Homebrew Analytics docs](https://docs.brew.sh/Analytics); [GitHub issue #142](https://github.com/Homebrew/brew/issues/142) (search summary)

**Restrictive scientific-software licences (the other pole)**
- **Gaussian:** licence terms bar competing developers and require written approval before publishing benchmarks. An anonymous "Banned by Gaussian" site listed affected scientists, and Gaussian's president publicly disputed its accuracy. This shows the community remembers and resents vendor control over the research process — [Wikipedia: Gaussian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_(software)); [Gaussian response](https://gaussian.com/?p=5261); [TACC Gaussian agreement](https://docs.tacc.utexas.edu/taccdocs/TACC_GAUSSIAN_Usage_Agreement.pdf) (search summary)
- **VESTA's licence** makes acknowledgement of figures a stated *condition* of use: an overt, legal-text route to the same goal — [VESTA licence mirror](https://data.gpo.zugaina.org/gentoo-zh/licenses/VESTA)

**Licence and law**
- **Crystal Builder is MIT-licensed** (`gh api`, 2026-11-06). MIT's conditions apply to copies of the *software*, not to its outputs. The GNU GPL FAQ likewise states that a program's output is generally not covered by the program's licence, unless the output contains parts of the program — [GPL FAQ](https://www.gnu.org/licenses/gpl-faq.html#WhatCaseIsOutputGPL) (not re-fetched)
- **GDPR Recital 30:** people "may be associated with online identifiers provided by their devices, applications, tools and protocols", such as IP addresses and cookie identifiers, and these can be used to profile and identify them. So a per-install or per-user ID embedded in files they share can be personal data — [gdpr-info.eu Recital 30](https://gdpr-info.eu/recitals/no-30/) (search summary)
- **ePrivacy Art. 5(3)** covers storing or accessing information on a user's terminal equipment whether or not it is personal data. EDPB draft guidelines (14 Nov 2023) read it broadly, including unique persistent identifiers and tracking pixels. Purely local use is outside scope only while the information never leaves the device — [Bird & Bird on EDPB draft](https://twobirds.com/en/insights/2023/global/draft-guidelines-on-the-scope-of-cookie-rules-edpbs-expansive-interpretation); [McDermott](https://www.mcdermottlaw.com/insights/eu-regulators-confirm-that-cookie-consent-rules-apply-to-much-broader-range-of-tracking-technologies/) (search summary; draft, final text not checked)

### Inferences
- **MIT compatibility.** Nothing in MIT forbids embedding a mark, and nothing obliges users to keep one: they may fork and delete it. So a mark can't be *enforced*, and its presence in the source is public. A "covert" mark in an open-source repository means a mark that is undocumented, not one that is secret. Anyone who reads `cif_writer.py`, the PNG writer or a diff will find it, and the Melissa/GUID and printer-dot stories show how such discoveries are framed: "hidden tracking". For a solo academic developer whose reputation *is* the project's adoption, that downside is large relative to the gain.
- **Privacy.** A **constant** mark (program name and version, identical for all users) identifies software, not people, and raises no GDPR issue. A **per-install or per-user** identifier turns every shared figure or file into a tracking token linking a researcher to their files. That engages GDPR (Recital 30) and arguably ePrivacy 5(3), and would need disclosure and a lawful basis. It is the Word-GUID pattern exactly. Avoid it.
- **Integrity.** Altering data values (Q4) is the clearest ethical line. Hidden pixel watermarks in figures are a grey zone: they are not a "feature" being added or removed in the JCB sense, but journals expect disclosure of processing. An undisclosed software-inserted signal could be challenged in image screening, and the user would carry the risk without knowing. I found no journal policy addressing this exact case (see Gaps).
- **Norm summary.** In this community the acceptable form of the developer's goal is "ask to be cited, make citing easy, and stamp provenance overtly in metadata fields designed for it". Covert marking has no precedent of acceptance in scientific software that I found.

### Gaps
- VS Code telemetry debates and the conda/Anaconda terms changes (2020 and 2024 enforcement on academic institutions) were not researched in this session.
- I did not retrieve CCDC/Mercury licence or telemetry terms, or VASP licence terms.
- I found no journal (Nature, ACS, RSC, IUCr) statement that specifically addresses software-embedded invisible watermarks in figures.
- The final (post-2023) EDPB Article 5(3) guidelines were not checked.

## Q6. Recommendation: the most effective and defensible approach, and plain risks of the covert options

### Takeaway
Do everything overt and stop there. Overt provenance in each export (which mostly exists for CIF already), plus a citable DOI or paper and an in-app "Cite" prompt, plus periodic literature and COD scans for the program name, will find more real usage than any hidden mark. It carries none of the integrity, privacy or reputational risk. Covert data fingerprints should be ruled out. Covert image watermarks are technically weak for this use case and, in an open-source repository, will be discovered.

### Cited Findings
(These rest on the findings above.)
- **Mentions are noisy and formal citations are the reliable signal**, so the highest-value step is a single citable reference and a `CITATION.cff` — [arXiv 2402.14602](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.14602); [FORCE11 principles](https://files.gitter.im/force11/force11-scwg/VpV6/peerj-cs-86-proof.pdf)
- **Overt figure acknowledgement in the licence works at scale for peers.** VESTA requires acknowledgement of figures, and OVITO asks for citation and reports more than 20,000 citing papers — [VESTA licence](https://data.gpo.zugaina.org/gentoo-zh/licenses/VESTA); [ovito.org](https://ovito.org/)
- **CIF audit fields are an accepted channel that Crystal Builder already uses**, and COD can be mined for it — local `xtal/io/cif_writer.py`; [COD](https://academic.oup.com/nar/article/40/D1/D420/2903497)
- **Invisible watermarks are fragile** even in the AI-image setting they were designed for — [WAVES](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.08573); [W-Bench](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.18775v2)
- **Hidden identifiers have a history of backlash** — [Melissa/GUID](https://www.networkworld.com/article/748462/security-melissa-virus-turning-15-age-of-the-stripper-still-unknown.html); [EFF printer dots](https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2017/06/printer-tracking-dots-back-news); [Audacity](https://www.techradar.com/news/audacity-reverses-opt-in-telemetry-plans-following-user-revolt)

### Inferences
Practical, defensible package, ranked by expected yield:
1. **A citable reference.** Add `CITATION.cff`, enable the Zenodo GitHub integration for a concept DOI, and in the medium term write a JOSS paper or a J. Appl. Cryst. computer-programs note. That is the venue where VESTA's paper lives and where crystallographers look.
2. **In-app citation prompts.** Help > "Cite Crystal Builder" with BibTeX and RIS. Add a dismissible one-line note in export dialogs ("If you publish this figure, please cite …") and include the citation text in the export folder or README. Optionally, follow VESTA and put a polite acknowledgement *request* (not a condition, to stay MIT-clean) in the README and About box.
3. **Overt metadata in every format, documented in the README and settable off in Preferences.** This is a product choice for Julius.
   - Keep the CIF `_audit_creation_method`, `_audit_creation_date` and `# Generated by` lines, and consider `_computing_molecular_graphics 'Crystal Builder x.y (doi)'`.
   - PNG: tEXt `Software` and `Source` keys, plus an iTXt `Comment` holding the DOI.
   - SVG: `<metadata>` with `dc:creator` and `dc:source`. PDF figures: XMP `CreatorTool`.
   - .xy/CSV: `#` header lines. XYZ: comment line. POSCAR: line 1. ORCA: `#` comment. Blender: a scene custom property.
   - Optionally a sidecar `provenance.json` or RO-Crate for bundles.
   - Never include a per-user or per-install ID.
4. **Measurement.** Set a Google Scholar alert for "Crystal Builder" and the DOI. Run periodic OpenAlex and Semantic Scholar queries for the DOI and the name, plus a periodic COD rsync and grep of `_audit_creation_method` for "Crystal Builder". This is the only scalable "detector" for a provenance mark that actually survives deposition.
5. **Optional overt visual identity.** A distinctive default render style, and an off-by-default (or clearly announced, easily disabled) small corner credit on image exports.

Plain risks of the covert options:
- **Hidden numeric fingerprints in data** alter the research record without the user's knowledge, which meets the plain-language definition of falsification and imposes integrity risk on users. They are also non-robust. **Do not do this.**
- **Hidden pixel watermarks in figures** have low recall after compositing, downscaling and PDF re-encoding. Detecting them needs bulk PDF harvesting and image extraction. They are discoverable in the source and would likely be framed as "secret tracking" (the GUID and printer-dot pattern). Journals expect processing to be disclosed. Expected value is negative.
- **Per-install identifiers in any mark** turn shared files into personal-data trackers (GDPR Recital 30 and ePrivacy 5(3)), with the strongest backlash precedents (Word GUID). **Do not do this.**
- **Undocumented but constant metadata** (just program name and version, not mentioned in the docs) has low practical risk, but there is no reason not to document it. Documenting it costs nothing and removes the "hidden" framing.

### Gaps
- Whether to disclose, whether metadata should default on or off, and whether to add a visible credit are Julius's product decisions. This note gives evidence, not a decision.
- I did not test empirically whether specific publishers keep PNG tEXt or SVG metadata in SI files.
